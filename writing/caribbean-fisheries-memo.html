Enhancing the Resilience of Caribbean Artisanal Fishers

2024

Marco Schürer, Goldman School of Public Policy

Policy Memo Climate Caribbean Fisheries

TO: Dr. Colin Young, Executive Director, CCCCC

FROM: Marco Schürer, Goldman School of Public Policy

DATE: July 7th, 2024

SUBJECT: Enhancing the Resilience of Caribbean Artisanal Fishers to Climate Change and Extreme Weather Events

The Overlooked Crisis

Caribbean artisanal fishers face an escalating crisis at the intersection of climate change and economic vulnerability. Rising sea surface temperatures, intensifying hurricanes, and coral reef degradation are fundamentally threatening the livelihoods of an estimated 182,000 fishers across the region. The economic toll is staggering: Hurricanes Irma and Maria alone caused an estimated $5.4 billion in fisheries-related damages across the Caribbean in 2017 (NOAA Fisheries, 2021). Yet artisanal fishers — who represent the majority of the region's fishing workforce and contribute significantly to food security — remain largely excluded from climate adaptation planning.

The vulnerability of these communities is compounded by their limited access to financial safety nets, inadequate early warning systems tailored to maritime activities, and the progressive degradation of the marine ecosystems upon which they depend. Coral reef loss alone threatens the habitat of over 65% of commercially important fish species in the region (Coker, Wilson, & Pratchett, 2014). Without targeted intervention, the Caribbean risks losing not only a critical economic sector but also the cultural heritage and food sovereignty that artisanal fishing sustains.

Policy Alternatives

Three policy alternatives have been evaluated based on their effectiveness in building long-term resilience, cost-efficiency, equity implications, and political feasibility.

Alternative 1: Maritime Early Warning Systems (MEWS)

Establish a network of localized early warning systems specifically designed for artisanal fishing communities. This includes SMS-based storm alerts, community radio broadcasts in local languages, and subsidized satellite communication devices for fishing vessels. The system would integrate with existing Caribbean Disaster Emergency Management Agency (CDEMA) infrastructure while adding fisher-specific marine weather forecasts and hazard alerts.

Alternative 2: Parametric Insurance and Livelihood Diversification

Develop a Caribbean-wide parametric insurance program for artisanal fishers, triggered by predefined weather thresholds (wind speed, rainfall). This would be paired with livelihood diversification grants supporting aquaculture, boat repair skills, and eco-tourism. The program would be administered through existing cooperative structures to minimize overhead and ensure equitable access (Dulal, Shah, & Ahmad, 2009).

Alternative 3: Community-Based Ecosystem Restoration and Capacity Building

Invest in community-led coral reef and mangrove restoration programs that simultaneously rebuild critical fish habitat and create local employment. This alternative includes training fishers in sustainable harvesting practices, establishing marine protected areas co-managed by fishing communities, and developing local capacity for climate adaptation planning. The approach leverages indigenous knowledge and builds social capital within fishing communities (FAO, 2018).

Comparison of Alternatives

Effectiveness Moderate Moderate High
Cost-Efficiency High Low Moderate
Equity Moderate High High
Political Feasibility High Moderate Moderate
Long-term Sustainability Low Moderate High

Recommendation

A phased implementation combining elements of all three alternatives offers the strongest path forward. In the immediate term (Year 1), deploy Maritime Early Warning Systems through existing CDEMA channels — this is the most cost-effective and politically feasible first step, providing tangible benefits to fishing communities while building institutional relationships. In the medium term (Years 2–3), pilot the parametric insurance program in two to three island nations with established fishing cooperatives, using lessons learned to refine the model before regional scaling. In parallel, launch community-based restoration programs in priority ecosystems identified by the UNDP's post-disaster assessments (UNDP, 2018).

The integrated approach addresses the fundamental limitation of each alternative in isolation: early warning systems save lives but do not rebuild livelihoods; insurance provides financial resilience but does not address ecosystem degradation; and community restoration builds long-term sustainability but cannot protect against immediate shocks. Together, they create a layered resilience framework that protects artisanal fishers across multiple time horizons.

Funding should be sourced through a combination of the Green Climate Fund, the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility (CCRIF), and bilateral development assistance. The World Bank estimates that every dollar invested in climate-resilient fisheries yields $4–7 in avoided losses and sustained economic activity (World Bank, 2020).

Call to Action

The Caribbean Community Climate Change Centre is uniquely positioned to convene regional stakeholders and champion this integrated approach. I recommend that the CCCCC:

(1) Commission a regional needs assessment of artisanal fishing communities' climate vulnerability, prioritizing the most exposed nations;

(2) Convene a multi-stakeholder working group including fisher cooperatives, national disaster management agencies, and international development partners to co-design the phased implementation framework;

(3) Submit a concept note to the Green Climate Fund by Q1 2025 for the MEWS pilot, leveraging the CCCCC's accredited entity status.

The window for proactive adaptation is narrowing. Each hurricane season without targeted intervention deepens the vulnerability of Caribbean artisanal fishers and erodes the region's capacity for self-sustaining food production. The cost of action is significant; the cost of inaction is far greater.
